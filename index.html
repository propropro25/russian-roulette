What the number means

Repeated small probabilities are not zero.

Under the simplifying assumption that every exposure has the same probability and that each exposure is independent, the probability of experiencing at least one event after k exposures is:

P(at least one event) = 1 − (1 − 1/N)ᵏ

Here, N is the denominator in “one in N,” and k is the number of exposures.

For example, a hypothetical risk of one in 100,000 repeated ten times produces an estimated cumulative probability of approximately 0.01%, assuming identical and independent exposures.

Real medical risks may not satisfy those assumptions. Risk can differ by product, dose number, age, sex, prior reaction, health status, interval, manufacturing period, outcome definition, and population.

Per dose or per person? Confirm what the denominator measures before repeating it.
Which population? A population average may not describe a particular subgroup.
Reported or confirmed? An event reported after exposure is not automatically established as caused by that exposure.
Point estimate or range? Preserve confidence intervals and uncertainty whenever available.
Same risk each time? Later exposures may not have the same probability as the first.
Overlapping outcomes? Different adverse outcomes should not be added unless their overlap and denominators are known.
Evidence rule: use product- and population-specific estimates from a traceable source. Passive surveillance reports may identify possible safety signals but generally cannot establish incidence or causation by themselves. Conversely, a surveillance system’s failure to identify an outcome does not establish that the outcome is impossible.
